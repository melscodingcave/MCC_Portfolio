Topic: Handling downstream effects
Date: 3Mo ago
Post: 
January 30, 2026 was a useful reminder of how interconnected production systems can be.

A change led to some unexpected downstream effects. Once identified, the focus shifted to understanding impact, stabilizing what we could, and making sure the response was well-documented.

How I approached the situation:
- Prioritized impact assessment before proposing fixes
- Helped validate and restore affected data where feasible
- Documented decisions, constraints, and follow-up actions
- Contributed to root cause analysis with a process-focused lens
- Ensured learnings were captured for future reference

The takeaway: issues happen even in well-run systems. What matters is responding with clarity, consistency, and accountability. Treating incidents as opportunities to strengthen processes is one of the most practical forms of engineering problem-solving.

Topic: My way of thinking

Working in QA — and spending a lot of time around data — taught me something early:

Outputs lie. Or at least, they mislead without context.
- A passing test doesn’t prove system resilience
- A clean dashboard doesn’t guarantee clean inputs
- A defect rarely starts where it surfaces
- “No alerts” isn’t the same as “no risk”

Titles aside, I’ve become less interested in the number and more interested in what produced it.
- What assumptions were made?
- What dependencies shifted?
- What edge case hasn’t been exercised yet?

Whether it’s a test result or a metric, the real engineering work is understanding the structure behind the output.

Trust doesn’t come from reports. It comes from knowing how the system behaves under stress.

Topic: Automation in a legacy system

I’ve been spending some time thinking through what it actually takes to add automation into a legacy codebase.

It’s one thing to automate something built with testing in mind. It’s another when the original design didn’t account for it.

The interesting questions aren’t about tools. They’re about approach:
- Where can you introduce seams without changing behavior?
- How do you improve visibility without overengineering it?
- What’s worth adapting — and what should remain untouched?

Legacy systems aren’t “bad.” They reflect decisions made under different constraints.

The challenge is layering in testability thoughtfully, without creating more complexity than you remove.

Automation at this stage isn’t about coverage numbers. It’s about understanding the structure well enough to introduce change responsibly.

That’s the kind of engineering problem I enjoy most.

Topic: Vibe Coding

I regularly double- and triple-check suggestions from tools like ChatGPT, Claude, and Copilot. This morning, while reading the TL;DR newsletter, I came across an article discussing a concept called “Comprehension Debt.” It immediately resonated.

Topic: The Best Route, not the Easiest
Date: 4Wks ago
Post: 
I've had a coworker who took the easy way out. Someone who would try to get others to do the work they were responsible for. Whether it was a lack of knowledge, experience, or confidence, I never had a chance to find out. 

It got me thinking about a question: 
- If you had to choose between the quickest/easiest route and the more time-intensive, detailed one, which would you pick? 
Most people seem to choose the former. 
If you asked me, my response wouldn't be so straightforward. 
The response, instead, would be a question: 
Which one is actually needed, and why? 

Most companies, especially with AI in the mix, are pushing for the quickest solutions. But 9 times out of 10, those aren't the best solutions. They often create downstream issues that better research upfront could have prevented.

Instead of asking "what's the fastest way to do this?", maybe we should be asking "what's the smartest and most efficient way?"

Efficiency doesn't always mean immediate speed. Sometimes it means investing weeks, or even months, into building something right, so you never have to do it again.

However, when you ask for that time, the response is often: "we can't afford it," instead of "how does this help us long-term?"

And that mindset it exactly where long-term problems begin.

Topic: Rewrite using AI
Date: 3Wks ago
Post: 
I was recently reading an article about letting engineers be engineers and using AI as a tool to help speed up the process. It focused on how AI is being handed the reins: writing code, making architectural decisions, even creating JIRA tickets.

The problem? No human actually owns those decisions.

The argument of "a senior engineer reviewed it" sounds good, but in reality, everything looks polished, so it gets approved and shipped. No real conversation. No discussion about whether it's actually the right approach for the team or the environment it's being built in. 

AI gives feedback based on generalized models. Not your team, your constraints, or your context. Not why you chose MSSQL over MongoDB, or AWS over Azure. Maybe those decisions were made because they're "technically" the best fit, BUT more often than not, they're based on what the team already knows. Maybe more people understand MSSQL. Maybe the team already works in AWS and doesn't want to spend weeks relearning Azure just because it's "the better path."

I agree with this completely. 

AI is a tool. It is a fantastic way to get ideas, increase speed and gain new perspectives, but it is NOT the architect. And it shouldn't be the final decision-maker. 

Ask it questions. Utilize its help. But most of all, own your work. 

If you're interested, here's the article: https://lnkd.in/e9qaUeb2. 

Tools don't replace ownership, people do.

Happy coding!

Topic: Passive and Adaptive Workouts
Date: May 8, 2026
Post: 
Why do workout apps still behave like spreadsheets with timers attached?

Seriously.

We have AI systems capable of interpreting language, analyzing patterns, and adapting to users over time… yet most fitness apps still require people to:
- unlock their phone between sets
- manually enter reps
- start rest timers
- navigate workout screens mid-session

And honestly, workouts are one of the worst environments for constant user interaction.
Your hands are sweaty.
You’re breathing hard.
You’re mentally focused on movement, recovery, or simply trying to finish the workout.

The more I think about it, the more it feels like fitness tech may be approaching the problem backwards.

Instead of building systems around manual data entry, what if workout systems became:
- passive
- adaptive
- conversational
- context aware

Not VR.
Not “AI screaming motivational quotes.”
Not replacing coaches.

More like:
- voice-first interaction
- automatic rest timing
- passive rep/set detection
- wearable telemetry
- movement pattern recognition
- fatigue and recovery modeling over time

Imagine a system where the first few weeks are user-guided while the system learns:
- pacing
- fatigue patterns
- recovery tendencies
- mobility limitations
- movement compensations
- progression capability
- even motivation patterns

Then eventually starts adapting in real time.

Not just:
“increase weight.”
But things like:
- “Your shoulder mobility has been declining lately. Want to add a quick mobility block before pressing today?”
- “You seem exhausted today. Want to reduce volume and focus on movement quality instead?”
- “Your performance trends show you can probably push harder today if you want to.”
- “You’ve skipped a few workouts. Want a shorter recovery-focused session instead?”

Not:
“AI replaces human coaching.”
More:
“the system quietly reduces friction, adapts to the user, and supports consistency over time.”

Feels like there’s a massive unexplored space between:
basic workout trackers
and
elite biomechanics labs.

Curious what people in fitness tech, wearables, biomechanics, AI systems, or UX think about where this space is heading over the next 5–10 years.
